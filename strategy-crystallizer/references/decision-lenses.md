# Decision lenses — how senior operators think

Thinking instincts that sharpen the **diagnosis** and the **gap questions**, not a checklist
to enumerate in the output. Internalize them; let them shape which constraint you name and which
question you ask. Curated for brand / marketing / business strategy (the UI- and code-specific
lenses from the source were dropped). Adapted from the "How great CEOs think" set in Garry Tan's
gstack `/plan-ceo-review`, which itself draws on Bezos, Munger, Grove, Jobs, Horowitz, Hastings,
Altman, and Rams.

Use them like this: when you write the **Diagnosis**, run the inversion reflex and proxy
skepticism. When you pick the **Choice / refusals**, run focus-as-subtraction and leverage. When
you size **ambition**, run reversibility and regret minimization.

1. **Inversion reflex (Munger).** For every "how does this brand win?", also ask "what would make
   it fail?" The failure answer often names the real constraint faster than the success answer.
   The strongest diagnosis is usually the inverse of the obvious goal.

2. **Reversibility × magnitude (Bezos — one-way vs two-way doors).** Classify the decision. Most
   moves are two-way doors (cheap to reverse) → bias to speed, just try it. Reserve the deliberation
   for the few irreversible, high-magnitude bets (a rebrand, a category redefinition, a price reset).
   Don't spend one-way-door rigor on a two-way-door choice.

3. **Focus as subtraction (Jobs — 350 products to 10).** The primary value-add is what to *not*
   do. This is the same muscle as Rumelt's refusals — a strategy is defined by what it rules out.
   If the plan can't name what it's sacrificing, it isn't focused, it's a wish list.

4. **Proxy / vanity-metric skepticism (Bezos Day 1).** Are the metrics still serving the goal, or
   have they become self-referential? Impressions, reach, "engagement," waitlist size — ask whether
   each one actually moves the business or just feels like motion. Name the metric that would
   actually prove the strategy is working (this feeds the Disconfirmation test).

5. **Status quo is the real competitor (Grove / YC).** The competitor is rarely the other brand —
   it's the customer's current cobbled-together workaround, or doing nothing. If the honest answer
   to "what do they do today instead?" is "nothing," the problem may not be painful enough to fund
   a strategy around. Diagnose against the workaround, not the named rival.

6. **Leverage obsession (Altman).** Find the input where small effort creates outsized output. In
   marketing that's usually a wedge channel, a single sharp message, or one owned asset — not a
   12-initiative spread. The highest-leverage move belongs in Coherent Actions; the rest belong in
   Refusals.

7. **Speed calibration (Bezos — 70% rule).** 70% of the information is usually enough to commit.
   If the strategy is waiting on certainty that won't arrive, that's a failure to face the
   challenge dressed up as diligence. Name what you'd decide *today* at 70%.

8. **Temporal depth / regret minimization (Bezos at 80).** Think in 3–10 year arcs for the brand,
   not this quarter. For a major bet, ask "which choice would they least regret looking back?" This
   is the lens that catches a plan that wins today and creates next year's nightmare.

9. **Wartime vs peacetime (Horowitz).** Diagnose the regime first. Peacetime (expand the market,
   grow the moat) and wartime (a single existential threat — a category collapse, a margin crisis,
   a platform shift) demand opposite playbooks. Applying peacetime moves in a wartime situation is
   a classic bad-strategy failure. Name which regime the brand is actually in.

10. **Willfulness (Altman).** The world yields to operators who push hard in one direction long
    enough; most give up too early. A strategy that hedges across three directions usually reflects
    unwillingness to commit. Pressure-test whether the brand is willing to be willful here — if not,
    say so, because a strategy nobody will commit to is a hope.

11. **Narrative coherence (Bezos).** A hard strategic call needs a legible "why," not consensus.
    The Crystallization sentence is this lens: if the argument behind the action isn't articulable
    in one line, the strategy isn't done. Make the why legible, not everyone happy.

---

**Ambition posture (set once, then commit — from gstack's scope modes).** Independent of BUILD /
AUDIT / COMPARE. Before filling the template, decide how hard to push scope, and hold it:

- **EXPAND** — "what would make this 10x better for 2x the effort?" Push the choice UP. Use for a
  brand with room to redefine its category or a founder who wants the cathedral.
- **HOLD** — take the current scope as given; make it bulletproof. Maximum rigor, no drift up or
  down. The default for an audit.
- **REDUCE** — find the minimum viable wedge that achieves the core outcome; cut everything else.
  Use when the brand is spread thin or the budget is small. Pairs with leverage + focus-as-subtraction.

And the standing permission, in every posture: **"scrap it and do this instead."** If there's a
fundamentally better approach than the one on the table, name it — don't optimize a doomed plan.
