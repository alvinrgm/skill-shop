# Grading Rubric — 7 dimensions out of 70

Every strategy this skill produces gets graded. Grading is the discipline
that prevents the skill from being just another "strategy generator" that
produces eloquent fluff.

Be honest. A generous C+ disguised as a B is worse than no grade at all.
The user wants the honest read, not the encouraging one.

---

## Scoring guidance per dimension (0–10)

### 1. Diagnosis sharpness

- **9–10**: Names a specific, non-obvious constraint. Uses concrete nouns.
  Would make the company's leadership uncomfortable. Could be wrong.
- **6–8**: Identifies the right area but is one level too abstract. Names
  a real problem but not the underlying mechanism.
- **3–5**: Names a symptom, not a constraint. "Awareness is low",
  "retention is weak" — true but unhelpful.
- **0–2**: No diagnosis present, or diagnosis is just a restatement of
  the goal ("we need to grow").

Common failure: producing a diagnosis that sounds smart but doesn't rule
anything out. A good diagnosis makes some strategies impossible and
others necessary.

### 2. Choice clarity (where to play)

- **9–10**: Names a specific arena, customer, occasion, and geography
  that excludes large categories of activity.
- **6–8**: Names the arena clearly but is broad on one dimension (e.g.,
  clear on customer, vague on geography).
- **3–5**: Names the arena in a way that doesn't meaningfully exclude
  anything — "we serve customers in our category."
- **0–2**: No choice expressed, or the choice is a goal ("we will be #1
  in our category").

### 3. Mechanism defensibility

- **9–10**: Names a structural advantage that competitors can't copy by
  spending more — density, network effects, partner exclusivity, process
  power, a cornered resource, branding earned over time, switching costs.
- **6–8**: Names an advantage that's real but partially copyable with
  enough investment and time.
- **3–5**: Names a tactic dressed up as a mechanism ("we have better
  creative", "we move faster").
- **0–2**: No mechanism, or the mechanism is just doing more of what
  competitors do.

This is where Helmer's 7 Powers framework is useful: scale economies,
network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding,
cornered resource, process power. If the strategy doesn't map to one
or more of these, the mechanism is probably weak.

### 4. Action coherence

- **9–10**: 3–5 actions that all reinforce the mechanism. Removing any
  one weakens the others.
- **6–8**: Actions are individually reasonable but loosely connected.
  Some could be dropped without harming the whole.
- **3–5**: A list of initiatives that don't visibly reinforce each other.
  Reads as "things to do" rather than "moves that compound."
- **0–2**: Too many actions (>5), or so vague the coherence can't be
  evaluated.

The dog's-dinner trap is the #1 failure mode here. If you can't argue why
each action multiplies the others' force, you have a roadmap, not a
strategy.

### 5. Refusal honesty

- **9–10**: 3–5 specific refusals that would create real political
  friction inside the organization. Names customers, markets, channels,
  or metrics the company is *choosing against*.
- **6–8**: Real refusals, but at least one is something the company
  wasn't going to do anyway.
- **3–5**: Vague refusals ("we won't be unfocused", "we won't chase
  every trend"). Refuses nothing real.
- **0–2**: No refusals listed, or the refusals are obvious ("we won't
  break the law").

The honesty test: read the refusals aloud as if presenting to the board.
If no one would push back, they're not real refusals.

### 6. Crystallization sharpness

- **9–10**: One sentence, ≤25 words, repeatable from memory after one
  reading. Implies the mechanism and rules things out. Falsifiable.
- **6–8**: Sharp idea but slightly too long, or two ideas fused that
  should be one.
- **3–5**: Corporate language ("unlock", "leverage", "drive growth"), or
  reads as a goal rather than a bet.
- **0–2**: Fluff. Could be any company in the space.

Test: would the user repeat this in the hallway without checking the doc?
If no, it's not crystallized yet.

### 7. Falsifiability (disconfirmation test)

- **9–10**: Names a specific leading indicator at a specific threshold
  that would force a rethink, with timing.
- **6–8**: Names the right indicator but is vague on threshold or
  timing.
- **3–5**: General "we'll monitor X" language that wouldn't actually
  force a change.
- **0–2**: No disconfirmation test, or a disconfirmation that's so weak
  it would always be passed.

## Letter grades

- **A+ (64–70)** — Ready to walk into the boardroom with. Every slot
  earns its place.
- **A (56–63)** — Strong. Minor sharpening on 1–2 dimensions.
- **B (48–55)** — Good bones, real work to do. Likely soft on
  diagnosis, refusals, or mechanism.
- **C (40–47)** — Has the shape of strategy but doesn't hold up to the
  tests. Probably mistaking goals for strategy in places.
- **D (<40)** — Wish list, not strategy. Start over from diagnosis.

For anything below A, output the top 3 specific improvements with
concrete suggestions. Don't be vague ("strengthen the mechanism") — say
exactly what to change ("the mechanism currently reads as a tactic;
reframe it around the partner co-marketing flywheel which is the
actual structural advantage").

## Calibration anchors (use these as mental reference points)

**A+ examples**: Walmart's 1970s "everyday low prices in small-town
America" with hub-and-spoke distribution. Apple under Jobs 1997
(radical focus on consumer end, kill 70% of SKUs). Southwest's
point-to-point single-aircraft-type model. Netflix's "spend more on
originals per sub than any competitor."

**C/D examples**: Yahoo under Mayer (dog's dinner of initiatives).
JCPenney under Ron Johnson 2011 (failure to face the core customer).
Quibi (marketing-around a product-market fit problem). Most
"customer-centric digital transformation" decks.
