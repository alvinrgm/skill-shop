# Examples — pattern recognition library

Use these as calibration anchors. When grading, ask "is this closer to
the Apple-under-Jobs side or the Yahoo-under-Mayer side?"

---

## A+ strategies (templates of what good looks like)

### Walmart, late 1970s

**Diagnosis**: Big retailers compete in cities and treat small towns as
afterthoughts. Small towns have stable demand, no major competitor, and
the population won't drive far for goods if a quality option exists
locally.

**Choice**: Towns of 5,000–25,000 in the rural south and midwest, one
town at a time.

**Mechanism**: Hub-and-spoke distribution centers that supply 100+
stores within a one-day drive, producing scale economies competitors
couldn't match without first matching the geographic density.

**Crystallization**: "Bring everyday low prices to small-town America
through hub-and-spoke saturation."

Why it's A+: Specific arena, defensible mechanism (chain-link system),
implicit refusals (not cities, not premium, not specialty), measurable.

### Apple under Jobs, 1997–2007

**Diagnosis**: Apple is bleeding to death from product proliferation and
losing the PC platform war to Windows. The fight to win general-purpose
computing is over and they lost.

**Choice**: The consumer end of premium personal computing and music,
not enterprise.

**Mechanism**: Tightly integrated hardware-software design that
Microsoft can't replicate (counter-positioning) and that customers can
feel.

**Coherent actions**: Kill 70% of products. Bet on iMac. Bet on iPod.
Bet on iTunes Store. Bet on retail stores. Bet on iPhone. Each move
reinforces the integrated-experience mechanism.

Why it's A+: Painful refusals (killed beloved products), structural
mechanism, coherent action sequence over a decade.

### Southwest Airlines, founding through 2000s

**Diagnosis**: The hub-and-spoke airline model produces high costs
through complexity (multiple aircraft types, complex routing, expensive
airports, baggage handling between flights).

**Choice**: Point-to-point routes between mid-sized US cities.

**Mechanism**: Single aircraft type (737 only) plus secondary airports
plus no baggage transfers, producing the lowest cost-per-seat-mile in
the industry. Process power competitors can't match without redesigning
their whole network.

Why it's A+: Every operational decision reinforces the mechanism. The
refusals (no international, no first class, no premium routes) are real
and politically painful.

## Cs and Ds (anti-patterns to recognize)

### JCPenney under Ron Johnson, 2011–2013

**Stated strategy**: "Fair and Square" pricing. Remove all coupons.
Reposition the brand for modern shoppers.

**Why it failed**: The diagnosis was wrong. JCPenney's core customer
wasn't waiting for an honest pricing experience. They were
coupon-clippers who got dopamine from finding a deal. Johnson projected
the customer he wished JCPenney had (the Apple customer) onto the
customer they actually had.

**Grade ≈ D**: Excellent execution of a strategy built on a misdiagnosis.

### Yahoo under Mayer, 2012–2017

**Stated strategy**: A list including Tumblr acquisition, Yahoo Screen,
Polyvore, Katie Couric, Yahoo Wallet, Flurry, brand refresh, vertical
launches, Saturday Night Live partnerships, Community revival, and a
dozen more initiatives.

**Why it failed**: Dog's dinner. No coherent argument for why these
actions reinforced each other. No refusals.

**Grade ≈ D**: Activity without strategy.

### Quibi, 2018–2020

**Stated strategy**: Premium short-form mobile-first video, $1.75B in
celebrity-led original content, 10-minute episodes.

**Why it failed**: Failure to face the challenge. The actual obstacle
was "nobody has indicated they'd pay for short-form video when YouTube
and TikTok are free." The strategy treated it as an awareness problem
when it was a fundamental demand problem.

**Grade ≈ D**: Treated a missing product-market fit as a marketing
problem.

### Pepsi, Kendall Jenner ad, 2017

**Stated strategy** (inferred): "Authentically connect with the cultural
conversation around social justice to drive brand relevance with Gen Z."

**Why it failed**: Fluff. The strategy was abstract enough that "have
Kendall Jenner hand a Pepsi to a riot cop" passed internal review. The
real challenge (declining soda consumption, no product innovation in
two decades) was unaddressed.

**Grade ≈ D**: Fluff + failure to face the challenge.

### Bud Light, 2023

**Stated strategy** (inferred): Standard influencer activation across a
broader cultural footprint.

**Why it failed**: Did not face the political reality of the actual
consumer base. When the partnership blew up, leadership refused to
commit either direction, lost both audiences, ceded #1 beer position
to Modelo.

**Grade ≈ C/D**: Failure to face the challenge, then a follow-on
failure to commit to a choice.

## Borderline cases (Bs that almost work)

### Allbirds, 2018–2022

**Diagnosis (correct)**: There's white space for sustainable footwear
that doesn't feel hippie.

**Mechanism (real)**: Natural materials brand built before competitors
saw the wave.

**Why it slipped**: Blue-sky objective ("inspire a more sustainable
future through innovation") ruled out nothing, so they expanded into
apparel, performance running shoes, and adjacent categories that
diluted the focus. Strategy was a B that became a C by failing to make
the refusals explicit.

### Peloton, 2019–2022

**Mechanism (real)**: Hardware + content + community subscription
flywheel that competitors couldn't replicate.

**Failure to face the challenge**: When COVID demand normalized in 2021,
the strategy didn't pivot from "we are scarce hardware" to "we are a
content business with hardware distribution." Stuck in the old
diagnosis past its sell-by date.

## Pattern recognition cheat sheet

When auditing a strategy, ask:

1. **Is there a diagnosis at all?** Most "strategies" skip this and start
   with actions. If there's no diagnosis, the grade ceiling is C.
2. **Does the diagnosis name a constraint or a goal?** "We need to grow"
   is a goal. "Our funnel breaks at second-order conversion because we
   never built habituation" is a diagnosis.
3. **Does the mechanism map to one of Helmer's 7 Powers?** If no, the
   mechanism is probably tactical and copyable.
4. **Are the refusals real?** Read them aloud as if to the board. Would
   anyone push back? If no, they're fake.
5. **Can the crystallization be repeated from memory after one read?**
   If no, it's not crystallized.
6. **Is there a falsifiability condition?** Most strategies have none.
   Adding one is the easiest way to go from B to A.
