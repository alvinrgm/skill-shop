# Rumelt Deep Reference

Load when the diagnosis is hard, when the user wants the full framework,
or when grading needs detailed reasoning.

---

## The kernel (the entire framework in three parts)

**1. Diagnosis** — What is actually going on? A good diagnosis simplifies
the overwhelming complexity of a situation by identifying certain aspects
as critical. It often uses analogy or metaphor. It names a constraint,
not an aspiration. It is uncomfortable to write because it usually
implicates people in the room.

**2. Guiding policy** — Given that diagnosis, what's our overall
approach? Not a goal. Not a tactic. A direction that channels action and
constrains choices. It says "we will focus on this, not that."

**3. Coherent action** — A set of specific, coordinated steps that all
carry out the guiding policy. The actions reinforce each other. They are
not a laundry list. Each one would be weaker without the others.

The kernel is the entire framework. Everything else (Lafley's cascade,
Helmer's 7 Powers, etc.) is supplementary structure that fits inside
these three slots.

## The four hallmarks of bad strategy (Rumelt's diagnostic toolkit)

These are the patterns to look for when grading or auditing.

### Fluff

Superficial abstraction dressed up as insight. "Our strategy is to
leverage our core competencies to deliver synergistic customer value
through a differentiated platform approach." The sentence has the shape
of meaning without the substance.

Tells: corporate adjectives ("synergistic", "customer-centric",
"world-class", "best-in-class", "purpose-driven", "innovative",
"transformational"). Long noun phrases. Words that could apply to any
company in any industry.

### Failure to face the challenge

Strategy that doesn't actually grapple with the obstacle. The diagnosis
is missing or wishful. The strategy pretends the hard part isn't hard.

Classic marketing manifestation: company has a product, retention, or
positioning problem, and marketing is asked to solve it as an awareness
problem because marketing has tools for awareness. The strategy that
results is well-built but pointed at the wrong target.

Examples:
- JCPenney under Ron Johnson removing coupons (refused to face that the
  core customer wanted the dopamine of a deal)
- Quibi (refused to face that nobody had asked for paid short-form video)
- Pepsi 2017 Kendall Jenner ad (refused to face that they had no product
  innovation since the 90s and were losing share to LaCroix)
- Bud Light 2023 (refused to face the political reality of their actual
  audience until it was a crisis)

### Mistaking goals for strategy

"Our strategy is to be the #1 player in our category." That is a goal.
The strategy would be the *how*. Companies confuse statements of desire
with plans of action.

Tells: "Our strategy is to [verb a goal]." Strategy is the mechanism, not
the destination. If the sentence answers "what do we want?" it's a goal.
If it answers "how do we get there?" it's strategy.

### Bad strategic objectives

Two variants:

- **Dog's dinner**: a long list of unprioritized initiatives that signals
  "do everything." 27 priorities means no priorities. Yahoo under Mayer
  is the canonical case.
- **Blue sky**: goals so vague they don't constrain action ("be a
  world-class organization"). Every "brand purpose" document since 2015
  is at risk of this.

Both fail to make the hard choice that strategy requires.

## Why bad strategy is so common

- Avoiding hard choices is more comfortable than making them
- Saying "we will focus on A and not B" creates losers and political
  enemies
- Leaders mistake motivation for strategy (Jack Welch's "stretch goals"
  critique)
- Templates and frameworks (SWOT, vision/mission docs) substitute for
  thinking
- Executive groupthink produces consensus that feels like alignment but
  is actually conflict avoidance
- Strategy work is uncomfortable; busywork is comfortable

This is why forcing the kernel discipline matters. The discomfort is
the work.

## Sources of strategic power (Rumelt's taxonomy)

When grading the mechanism slot, check whether the strategy is drawing on
one of these:

- **Leverage** — anticipation, insight into pivot points, applying
  concentrated force at the right moment
- **Proximate objectives** — chunking work into achievable near-term
  targets that build toward the big goal
- **Chain-link systems** — where strength is determined by the weakest
  link, and competitors can't copy by fixing one link
- **Design** — tightly integrated systems where parts reinforce each
  other (Apple)
- **Focus** — concentrating resources where you have the most leverage
- **Growth** — from real strength, not just expansion
- **Advantage** — real, defensible, asymmetric advantage
- **Dynamics** — surfing waves of change rather than fighting them
- **Inertia and entropy** — exploiting competitors' organizational
  inertia
- **Putting it all together** — coherent action that uses multiple
  sources of power simultaneously

If none of these is present, the mechanism is probably weak. The strategy
is hoping to win by trying harder, which usually doesn't work.

## Helmer's 7 Powers (the modern moat taxonomy)

Use when grading mechanism defensibility. A real moat maps to one or
more of these:

1. **Scale economies** — costs fall as volume rises (faster than
   competitors')
2. **Network economies** — product gets more valuable as more people use
   it
3. **Counter-positioning** — new business model the incumbent can't copy
   without cannibalizing themselves
4. **Switching costs** — customers face real cost to leave
5. **Branding** — trust and habit earned over time, can't be bought
6. **Cornered resource** — exclusive access to a critical input (talent,
   IP, geography, partnership)
7. **Process power** — operational know-how that's hard to replicate
   even with full visibility

If the strategy's mechanism doesn't map to any of these, it's not durable.

## The single sentence to hold

**Strategy is coherent action backed by an argument.**

If you can't articulate the argument, you don't have a strategy. You
have a hope.
